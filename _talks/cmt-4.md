## Résumé

In Sub-Saharan Africa, armed conflicts extend beyond military engagements to violently deconstruct healthcare systems. In Dschang, the massive influx of Internally Displaced Persons from neighboring Anglophone regions, coupled with chronic underfunding, has saturated public health facilities. For marginalized populations—specifically LBQ women and displaced sex workers—the formal healthcare system has shifted from a place of healing to a space of stigma and danger. This qualitative action-research, conducted by Association ESPOIR, examines how institutional collapse reinforces patriarchal barriers. Through the analysis of 200 patient files and 15 in-depth interviews, the study reveals an erosion of confidentiality and a retreat into conservative norms. In times of crisis, access to safe abortion and sexual and reproductive health (SRH) care for minorities is actively obstructed by increased community surveillance and disrupted supply chains for emergency kits (PEP). To address this institutional failure, Association ESPOIR has implemented health self-defense mechanisms. Supported by our strategic partner, the Global Fund for Women, we are already working on the ground to provide life-saving alternatives. Our "Resilience Clinic" model—utilizing secure, mobile units—provided SRH services to 450 women over 18 months, drastically reducing deaths related to unsafe abortions in our intervention zone. We argue that health resilience in conflict zones requires a "feminization" of care that acknowledges health as a political battlefield. It is urgent to formalize partnerships between grassroots feminist organizations and public health authorities to establish "protected care zones." Without specific protection mechanisms for sexual minorities, the healthcare system becomes an active participant in war-related violence.
